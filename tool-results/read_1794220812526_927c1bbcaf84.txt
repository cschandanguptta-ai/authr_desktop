    58→## [Heading1] 1. Executive Summary
    59→Authr OS 2.3 is a multi-agent authoring workstation: six Weaver sub-agents (Executive Orchestrator, Plot Architect, Character Psychologist, World Builder, Scene Composer, Continuity Manager) run over a dynamic multi-provider LLM orchestrator, exposed through two workspaces — the Interactive Wizard for step-by-step co-writing and the Autonomous Studio for hands-free background production. Version 3.0 turns this workstation into a one-person author publisher: a single writer who plans, drafts, serializes, narrates, and ships finished products to retail and pay-per-chapter platforms without leaving the application.
    60→The v3.0 programme stands on four pillars. First, a writer-sovereign six-layer drafting engine in which every generated artifact — bible records, outlines, beats, prose, style passes — is a proposal that becomes canon only when the writer confirms it. Second, serialized fiction as a first-class project format, available in both workspaces and specified end to end by the Serialized Fiction Blueprint 2.0 (supplied with this revision): deterministic validators for the opening hook, the seven-beat chapter model, chapter-ending quality, a pre-draft worksheet, and a 14-point pre-publication scorecard. Third, a studio-grade audiobook engine built on Pocket TTS as the local synthesis engine, with IPA pronunciation, cross-segment acoustic continuity, a three-layer ffmpeg mastering chain, ACX compliance auditing, and SMIL read-along export. Fourth, publish-ready output: EPUB 3 for Kindle, print interiors with a verified gutter ladder, and paste-ready chapter packages for serial platforms.
    61→This document is Revision 2 of the improvement plan. Rev 1 contained four scope decisions that are corrected here before implementation begins: scene auto-drafting is retained (not removed) behind a writer-confirmation gate; the feature actually being removed is the Scene Automation & Video tab in Interactive Mode; the local TTS engine is Pocket TTS (replacing Kitten TTS); and web novel serialization is added to both modes and promoted from the post-P5 backlog into the main roadmap as phase P3. Table 1 summarizes the corrections.

    84→## [Heading1] 2. Product Vision & Guiding Principles
    85→The product thesis is the one-person author publisher. A single writer operates as an entire publishing house: editorial (bibles, outlines, beats), production (prose, style passes), serialization (chapter packaging and cadence), audio (narration and mastering), and distribution (retail and platform exports). Authr OS 3.0 supplies the staff — six agents, validators, ledgers, and exporters — while the writer remains the sole editor-in-chief. Every design decision in this plan is tested against a single question: does it increase what one writer can plan, produce, and publish without giving up creative control?
    86→Principle 1 — Writer sovereignty. The application keeps the v2.3 contract “AI proposes, human approves & refines” and hardens it into a data-level rule: no text enters the manuscript canon without an explicit writer_confirmed flag. Rev 2 restates this deliberately: retaining the Level 4 Scene Auto-Drafter is safe precisely because its output is a proposal that the writer opens, edits, and confirms — auto-drafting accelerates the writer; it never replaces the writer's signature.
    87→Principle 2 — One manuscript, many formats. The same confirmed manuscript source flows to every output: EPUB 3 and print interior for retail, chapter packages for serial platforms, and the audiobook pipeline for audio. Serialization is not a separate product; it is a view and a validation profile over the same project container.
    88→Principle 3 — Local-first with escape hatches. Where a task can run locally, it does: Pocket TTS synthesizes narration on-device, validators and ledgers are pure local computation, and the .authr container keeps the whole project portable. Cloud LLM providers and cloud TTS remain configurable per agent for scale and quality, never as a hard dependency.
    89→Principle 4 — Deterministic guardrails around stochastic engines. Language models propose; deterministic code decides what passes. Cliché detection, hook validation, ending-type classification, seven-beat coverage, scorecard triggers, and the Chekhov ledger are all implemented as local, testable, versioned checkers. This is what makes the guardrails auditable: the same chapter always produces the same audit result.
    90→Principle 5 — Publish-ready, not draft-ready. An export is only done when it passes the destination's own gate: epubcheck for Kindle files, the KDP Print Previewer for interiors, the ACX audio meter for audiobooks, and the serialization validator suite for chapter packages. Internal success metrics are defined against those external gates, not against internal impressions.

   104→## [Heading1] 3. Revision 2 Scope Corrections
   105→Rev 1 of this plan misread two requirements and deferred a third that belongs in the core. This section is the authoritative scope baseline: four corrections, numbered SCR-1 through SCR-4. Nothing else in the Rev 1 plan changes. Because implementation has not started, these corrections carry zero rework cost; they are made now, before P0 begins.
   106→## [Heading2] 3.1 SCR-1 — Scene Auto-Drafting Retained at Level 4
   107→Rev 1 removed the Level 4 auto-expansion step — the component that turns confirmed beats into full prose scenes — and proposed Writer Studio and a Live AST Annotator as replacements. That was a misreading of the requirement. Rev 2 retains the Level 4 Scene Auto-Drafter as the productivity core of the engine. The sovereignty concern that motivated the removal is solved by a gate, not by amputation: every L4 draft is written into the chapter workspace with status PROPOSED; it becomes canon only when the writer opens it, edits or accepts it, and taps Confirm Chapter, which sets writer_confirmed = 1. Unconfirmed drafts never feed the continuity ledger, the bibles, or any export.
   108→Writer Studio and the Live AST Annotator (section 4) are therefore complementary surfaces, not replacements: the Studio is where the writer reads, edits, and confirms auto-drafted chapters, and the Annotator paints quality and continuity signals over confirmed text. For the target user — a solo author sustaining two to three released chapters per week on a serial cadence — removing auto-drafting would collapse exactly the throughput v3.0 exists to protect.
   109→## [Heading2] 3.2 SCR-2 — Removed: Scene Automation & Video Tab (Interactive Mode)
   110→The feature actually being removed is the Scene Automation & Video tab in Interactive Mode. In v2.3 this screen is a vertical-video generator: the user uploads still images, sets a display time per image (1–30 s), optional Ken Burns slow zoom and per-image focal points, an aspect ratio (9:16 default), and neon equalizer overlays; the feature then muxes the project's synthesized audio with the image slideshow into a 1080×1920 video (YouTube Shorts / long-form audiobook video), optionally auto-splitting long-form output and exporting an SRT subtitle sidecar. Rendering runs through the RxFFmpeg binding and exports to the system media store.
   111→Removal rationale, in priority order: (1) product focus — v3.0 is a publishing platform for writers; social-video marketing is a different product with a different audience; (2) maintenance cost — the video path is the single largest ffmpeg surface in the app (codec muxing, resolution handling, focal-point animation, equalizer rendering), and it is the least exercised path in the desktop port; (3) opportunity cost — the engineering weeks it consumes in every release are redirected to serialization and publishing, which sit directly on the revenue loop of the target user. Note what is not removed: the ffmpeg audio DSP chain (sidechain ducking, EBU R128 normalization, true-peak limiting) is kept in full — audiobook mastering depends on it (section 7.3) — and image handling for EPUB covers is unaffected.

   137→## [Heading2] 3.3 SCR-3 — Local TTS Engine: Kitten TTS Is Replaced by Pocket TTS
   138→v2.3 ships Kitten TTS in two forms: a cloud client that drives a free shared Gradio HF Space over a queue-and-poll SSE protocol with retry, and a local native library (libkittentts-native.so, 762 KB) that exists only for the armeabi-v7a ABI. The practical defects are structural: the cloud form depends on a shared space with no availability guarantee; the local form is English-only, ships no arm64 build, and offers no voice cloning. Rev 2 replaces both with Pocket TTS (Kyutai Labs, MIT licence): a 100M-parameter local engine that runs in real time on a CPU, clones a narrator voice from a short WAV reference, streams unbounded text, and covers seven core languages with community models for twelve more, including Chinese, Japanese, Hindi, and Korean. Section 7 carries the full specification and engine matrix; the integration cost is bounded because v2.3 already abstracts TTS engines behind a manager interface.
   139→## [Heading2] 3.4 SCR-4 — Web Novel Serialization Added to Both Modes
   140→Rev 1 parked serial platforms behind P5 as a post-release extension. Rev 2 promotes serialized fiction to a first-class project format, implemented in phase P3, available in both the Interactive Wizard and the Autonomous Studio. Two facts drive the change. First, the product input for this revision — the Serialized Fiction Blueprint 2.0, which supersedes the 1.0 draft Rev 1 referenced — specifies the structural, pacing, and retention mechanics of pay-per-chapter serialization in implementable detail (section 6 encodes it end to end). Second, v2.3 already seeds the data model: Book carries isSerialized, targetChapters (default 100), and releaseSchedule (default “Mon/Wed/Fri”), and the Outline and Drafting workers already branch on isSerialized. The remaining distance to a complete format — validators, the seven-beat anatomy meter, ending advisory, the pre-draft worksheet, the pre-publication scorecard, micro-arc planning, buffer and cadence tooling, platform export profiles — is squarely inside v3.0 scope.

   204→## [Heading1] 5. Prose Quality Systems: Cliché Cleaner & Chekhov Ledger
   205→Two deterministic systems bracket the stochastic engine: the cliché cleaner runs before and after generation (suppressing patterns when the model proposes them and linting them out of confirmed text), and the Chekhov ledger runs after confirmation, accounting for every narrative promise the text makes. Both are local, versioned, and unit-tested; neither depends on any cloud service.
   206→## [Heading2] 5.1 Cliché Cleaner
   207→The cleaner ships 154 patterns across six classes, each with a severity (info, warn, error), a plain-language explanation, and in most cases a suggested rewrite. Patterns are regular expressions compiled once per project open. During generation, patterns are injected into the drafting prompt as hard constraints for every provider, and where the provider's API supports token biasing, the most frequent offender tokens are additionally suppressed at decode time. After confirmation, the linter re-scans the frozen text so the AST can render hits inline in the Writer Studio.

   254→## [Heading2] 5.2 Chekhov Continuity Ledger
   255→The ledger operationalizes the “gun on the wall” rule: every promise the text makes — a weapon described in detail, a debt, a skill, a named grudge, an unanswered question — is a ledger entry with a lifecycle. L6 extracts candidate guns from confirmed text (proposal by LLM, confirmation deterministic against the extraction rules), and the writer confirms or rejects each entry in the Studio rail. Guns the writer confirms then move through a strict state machine; the final-act recovery gate blocks a project's compile step while any confirmed gun is still unfired, so no manuscript ships with an orphaned promise unless the writer explicitly waives it with a note.

   282→## [Heading1] 6. Web Novel Serialization Format (Both Modes)
   283→Serialized fiction is a distinct economic product: readers pay per chapter in a micro-transaction loop, discover the story through a free opening tier, and stay through cadence and habit. The Serialized Fiction Blueprint 2.0 supplied with this revision specifies the structural, pacing, and retention mechanics of that model in implementable detail, and it is materially different from the 1.0 draft Rev 1 referenced: its core principle is that retention is built through character attachment, narrative progression, emotional investment, suspense, and meaningful payoffs — not through cliffhangers alone. v3.0 encodes Blueprint 2.0 as a project format (section 9) with a validator suite, a pre-draft worksheet, a pre-publication scorecard, a micro-arc planner, and a retention toolkit, all available in both workspaces. Every structural rule in this section is checked by deterministic local code; the LLM drafts, but code decides what passes.
   284→The format extends what v2.3 already seeds. The Book entity carries isSerialized, targetChapters (default 100), and releaseSchedule (default “Mon/Wed/Fri”), and the Outline and Drafting workers already branch on isSerialized. Section 6.12 defines the full field set; Appendix A carries the DDL.
   285→## [Heading2] 6.1 The Core Retention Principle
   286→Blueprint 2.0 opens with a contract every serial chapter must satisfy, and v3.0 makes it the first screen of the serial workspace so the writer sees it on every chapter card. Every chapter must (1) give the reader something worthwhile for the time invested, (2) change the situation, understanding, relationships, or stakes, and (3) create a compelling reason to continue reading. These three duties are enforced at three different points: duty 1 and 2 are scored by the pre-publication scorecard (6.9), and duty 3 is the ending check (6.4). The principle also disciplines the product itself: nothing in the serialization tooling is allowed to push the writer toward shock-value endings at the expense of narrative credibility — the blueprint's ultimate rules (6.9) appear verbatim in the export pre-flight dialog.
   287→## [Heading2] 6.2 Opening Strategy: Capturing the Reader (Chapters 1–5)
   288→The opening benchmark in Blueprint 2.0 is deliberately softer than the 1.0 draft's hard 200-word rule: introduce a compelling source of interest within the first 200–400 words, and avoid opening with extended geography, history, family background, or fictional-system exposition unless it directly serves the immediate scene. The hook validator therefore scans the opening span for any of seven acceptable sources of interest — a disturbing or unusual event; an urgent problem; a character pursuing something important; an unexpected confrontation; a mystery that challenges the reader's assumptions; a powerful emotional situation; an intriguing voice or unusual narrative perspective — and flags openings that run on pure description. The drafting rule is encoded into the L4 prompt for chapter one: begin with a character experiencing, pursuing, or confronting something that matters, letting world-building emerge through action and observation.

   305→## [Heading2] 6.3 The Seven-Beat Chapter Model
   306→Blueprint 2.0 replaces the 1.0 draft's fixed 10/70/20 anatomy with a seven-beat model — a flexible narrative progression, not a rigid template. The beats are functions of the chapter, not mandatory scene divisions: a beat may occupy one line or half the chapter, and quiet chapters may weight them very differently. L3 beat cards for serial chapters are pre-seeded with the seven beats as prompts, and the coverage checker verifies on confirmed text that each function is performed somewhere in the chapter; missing functions raise a warning the writer can address or waive with a note.

   351→## [Heading2] 6.4 Chapter Endings: Six Types, Selected Naturally
   352→Blueprint 2.0 expands the ending catalogue from four tiers to six types and, critically, prohibits mechanical rotation: the ending must be selected according to the chapter's dramatic development, must arise naturally from preceding events, and the same high-intensity type must not be used repeatedly. The 1.0 rotation wheel and its least-recently-used suggestion are therefore removed from the design; in their place the ending advisor classifies the closing passage against the six types, tracks type history, and raises a soft fatigue flag only when the same high-intensity type repeats in consecutive chapters. Quiet, reflective, or emotionally satisfying endings are explicitly supported — the classifier recognizes them instead of forcing every chapter into a shock category.

   354→Type
   355→Mechanism
   356→Blueprint example
   357→Signature signals

   382→The advisor applies the blueprint's six selection principles as product rules: it never auto-assigns a type; it checks that the ending's stakes are understandable and that the unresolved question matters to the protagonist or central plot; it warns when consecutive high-intensity endings repeat the same type; and it cross-references the promise ledger so endings that promise answers the following chapters do not deliver are surfaced at the next chapter's drafting time. The objective rule, quoted in the product, is that an effective chapter ending does not always need to shock — a character's decision, an unexpected emotional realization, or a fulfilled promise is often more compelling.
   383→## [Heading2] 6.5 Micro-Arc Architecture: the 10–15 Chapter Cycle
